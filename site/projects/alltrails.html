Case Study — 01

AllTrails
Group Hike

Mobile App Feature Design UX Research
Role Primary Designer
Year 2023
Duration 4 Weeks
Tools Figma, ProtoPie, Adobe Suite
Type Feature Design

Overview

01

A solo school project completed over 4 weeks in 2023. AllTrails is an outdoor enthusiast's companion, empowering users to explore hiking, running, and biking trails with essential information like weather updates, navigation guidance, and user reviews. This project focused on the seamless integration of a new Group Hike feature to further enhance the outdoor experience and foster community among its 6.5 million registered users.

Problem Statement

02

App Store and Google Play reviews surfaced a recurring theme: AllTrails users wanted to connect with like-minded outdoor enthusiasts for group experiences, but the platform offered no way to organise, join, or coordinate group activities. This left a gap between the app's individual trail discovery tools and the community-driven spirit of outdoor adventure.

Research & Analysis

03

Research drew on App Store and Google Play reviews, along with posts from r/vancouver and outdoor hiking communities on Reddit. Three consistent themes emerged. Vancouver users described the city as socially difficult to break into, making it hard to find hiking companions organically. Wildlife concerns came up repeatedly, with bears mentioned specifically. Many users noted they felt more confident hiking with others present. Fitness level mismatch was also a common frustration. Users wanted to hike with people at a similar pace rather than holding the group back or being left behind.

This was supplemented with a competitor analysis of the two closest rivals in the space.

Gaia GPS Trail finding, route recording, weather forecast. No group hike option, representing a clear gap in the market.
Hiking Project Crowd-sourced, North America focused. Features "Gems" (points of interest) and trail popularity sorting. Free, no group feature.

User Flow

06

Design Decisions

07

The feature was built around two complete flows layered on top of AllTrails' existing trail discovery experience.

The Host flow ran across four steps plus a success state: date, time and activity selection; group size; public or private event decision; confirmation and posting. The public path terminated at a community-posted success state. The private path terminated at a code-and-share success state. Both success states included a join code, social share options, and a route to event management.

The Join flow covered the browse tab with search and code entry, an event detail sheet showing trail photo, metadata, and occupancy ratio, and a one-tap join action routing to Saved Events.

Supporting screens included the Community feed (Local tab showing nearby events posted by others with a follow mechanic), the Events tab (Host/Join sub-navigation), and the Saved Events screen (with Lists, Downloads, and Events tabs, status pills for Upcoming and Past, and an empty state).

The most non-obvious decision was splitting the public and private paths at Step 3 into distinct terminal states. The original design combined "Get a link" and "Post to community page" on a single sharing screen. This felt simpler to build but created a semantic problem. A host who chose "private" was being shown community posting options that contradicted their stated intent. It also collapsed two fundamentally different distribution jobs into one UI. The two-path model was chosen because a public host wants community discovery while a private host wants direct coordination. Giving each its own terminal state meant each success screen could be precise rather than generic.

Iterations

08

The most significant iteration was on Step 1 of the host flow, specifically the date, time, and activity inputs.

The original design used free-text fields. The user typed a date, typed a time, and selected an activity from an unlabelled dropdown with no visible state. This approach was flagged as an error source. Free-text date and time input on mobile generates formatting errors, requires validation logic, and creates uncertainty about what the field accepts.

The iterated version replaced all three with native inputs: a date picker pill with chevron, a time picker pill with chevron, and a labelled segmented control for activity type. Each iteration surfaced new details to address. The chevron was initially only on the time pill, creating an asymmetric affordance. The segmented control had a pre-selected state (Hiking with white fill) that contradicted the intended unselected default. Each issue was caught and corrected across successive rounds.

The final version delivered both pills with consistent editability signalling and the segmented control in a true unselected default state. Neither option has fill until the user makes an explicit choice.

Visual Design

09

I developed a style guide aligned with AllTrails' existing brand identity while giving the new feature its own visual language. The guide defined primary and secondary colour palettes and a typography system using Beatrice, chosen for its modern, legible character across heading and body sizes.

Heuristic Evaluation

12

Following the high-fidelity designs, an expert design review was conducted against Nielsen's 10 Heuristics across all 13 screens, covering the host flow, join flow, community feed, and saved events. The evaluation surfaced 14 findings. Five were rated S3 (Major, impacts task completion) and represent the highest-priority gaps before a development handoff.

The nine S2 findings are addressable in a subsequent design pass without blocking development. These include time format inconsistency, unlabelled share icons, and the absence of a repeat-host shortcut. The core host and join flows are structurally sound. The remaining work is failure state coverage: designing for what happens when things go wrong, not just when they go right.

Reflection

13

This project was an opportunity to run a full UX process within a tight four-week timeline, spanning secondary research and competitor analysis through to high-fidelity design and expert review. The Group Hike feature emerged directly from real user feedback in app store reviews and community forums. Each design decision was grounded in genuine need rather than assumption.

The heuristic evaluation that followed was a valuable exercise in critical self-review. It confirmed that the core flows are structurally sound while naming a clear next priority: error state coverage. Designing only for the happy path is a common early-stage pattern, and having those gaps surfaced explicitly provided a concrete roadmap for the next design pass.
