Service Hub, a trusted home services marketplace for adults 65+ and their caregivers

Overview

01

Service Hub is a mobile home services marketplace built for adults 65 and older and for the adult children who help them. Seniors use it to book plumbers, electricians, handymen, and other local pros. Their family caregivers use it to see what is happening in their parent's home, even from another city.

The product is built around one idea. Trust is not a feature you add later. It is the whole architecture. Every screen, badge, and piece of copy was tested against a single question. Does this make a senior feel safer letting a stranger into their home?

Design principle: A senior should feel as confident letting a Service Hub pro into their home as they would with a neighbor's recommendation.

The Problem

02

75% of adults over 50 want to stay in their current home as long as possible, per AARP. Yet no major home services app is built for them. The dominant platforms were designed for younger, tech-confident users. The 65+ group was never the target, not in the interaction model, not in the trust layer, and not in the pricing.

At the same time, older Americans lose an estimated $81.5 billion a year to financial fraud. Home repair scams are one of the top ways that loss happens. The existing apps do not solve this. They add to it.

01

Designed for the wrong user

Hourly bids, in-app chat, and digital dispute forms assume a user who is fluent with mobile apps. Many seniors are not. They want a phone call. They want a clear price. They want a person on the other end, not a chatbot.

02

No real trust infrastructure

TaskRabbit holds a 1.5 star rating with the Better Business Bureau and 364 complaints logged in the last three years. Background checks are shallow. Workmanship guarantees are written to protect the platform, not the customer. Seniors are left exposed.

03

No place for the caregiver

Adult children often help make service decisions for aging parents. They have zero visibility into what happens in that home today. No booking record. No pro photo. No way to see if the job actually went through.

Market & Users

03

The demographic wave is already here. By 2030, 71.6 million Americans will be 65 or older. That is up from 61.2 million today. Their homeownership rate is 79%, which is 14 points higher than the national average. They control a growing share of home spending.

The owner-occupied remodeling market will hit $509 billion in 2025. Adults 55 and older account for more than 56% of that spending. With mortgage rates holding near 6.5%, existing-home sales are at a 30-year low. Seniors are not moving. They are staying put and fixing up. AARP's AgeTech Collaborative has grown to 600+ companies and 90+ venture partners since 2021. Capital is starting to follow the demographics. The window for a category-defining senior home services platform is narrow. Two to four years.

Competitor landscape

Every major competitor serves part of the need. None of them serve this user well.

TaskRabbit App-only marketplace. No phone booking. No human support line. 1.5 star BBB rating and hundreds of unresolved complaints. Trust signals are generic.
Angi and HomeAdvisor Lead generation model. The platform sells your contact info to pros. Settled with the FTC in 2025 over misleading practices. Vetting is inconsistent.
GoGoGrandparent Phone concierge service for seniors. Strong idea. Thin home maintenance offering. High service fees. Limited national coverage.
Papa Sold through health insurers rather than direct to seniors. Faced abuse allegations reported by Bloomberg in 2023. Not built for self-serve home repair bookings.

The two people the product serves

Research pointed to two users who share one job. Getting a trusted pro into the home, without anyone losing control.

  • Worries about being overcharged or pressured into extra work
  • Gets anxious when an app shows vague status messages

  • Step in fast if something looks wrong, without overriding her
  • See billing clearly so he can spot odd charges early

Pain Points

  • Coordinates from 600 miles away with no live visibility today
  • Fears a pro may not show up or may charge for work not done
  • Worries any oversight feature will upset his mother

Design Principles

04

Six principles guided every decision in the product. They came straight out of the research and the competitor analysis.

01

Trust is the architecture

Every screen, badge, and line of copy was judged against one question. Does this make a senior feel safer letting this person into their home?

02

Specificity over assertion

"Insured, $1M liability, OR PLB-28841" can be checked. "Verified" cannot. Trust badges should cite real details, not make vague claims.

03

Senior autonomy first

Every caregiver feature is opt in and controlled by the senior. The senior sets the permission. The caregiver operates inside it.

04

Design for two users at once

Every feature was tested against both personas. If it helped the senior but left the caregiver out, or the other way around, it was redesigned.

05

Prevent errors, do not just fix them

Fixed pricing, price confirmation before booking, and a short undo window remove the conditions that cause mistakes. This follows Nielsen's fifth usability heuristic.

06

Lower the decision cost

Fewer choices at each step, guided by Hick's Law. Pre-vetted recommendations instead of open bidding. Quick-tap common issues instead of free text. A one-tap option to book the same provider again.

Dual-User Architecture

05

The product's core idea is two users with one shared job. Each one gets an interface built for what they need. Both sides share a single trust layer underneath.

The senior interface Role selection as the first screen. OTP login by phone number, so no password to forget. A three-step profile setup with a privacy note on step one. Large tap targets with icon-led service categories. Provider lists with face photos, verified badges, and review counts. A "Call the pro" button before any commitment. Fixed price shown before booking. Payment only charged after the senior marks the job done. A day-of reminder phone call at 9 AM. A two-minute undo window on cancellations.
The caregiver interface A separate onboarding path: "I am helping a family member." An access request flow that only connects once the senior approves. A dashboard with the senior's home name, address, and recent job stats. Live status on every booking. Provider photos and badges visible on every job card. Clear pricing like "$200 ($40 per hour)." Approve and cancel actions on pending bookings. An alerts tab for anything needing a human decision. All permissions set by the senior, not the caregiver.

Key Design Decisions

06

Four decisions define the product. Each one maps to a specific finding from research, a competitor gap, or a rule from classic usability work.

01

Specific credentials instead of generic badges

An early version showed "Insured" as a plain label. The final version shows "Insured, $1M liability" and "Licensed, OR PLB-28841." Seniors can actually look these up. This is how real word-of-mouth trust works. It names a specific person and a specific fact.

02

Caregiver approval is opt in and senior controlled

The "Require their approval to book" toggle is off by default. It is framed as a choice the senior makes. Seniors who fear losing independence will not adopt a product that feels like surveillance. Making it a gift from the senior removes that barrier.

03

Fixed price shown before the user commits

Open-ended hourly rates are the main mechanism in home repair fraud against seniors. The booking flow shows a fixed or estimated total before the confirm button. The caregiver dashboard shows both the total and the rate type, for example "Est. total $200 ($40 per hour)," so a family member can sanity-check it from anywhere.

04

Two-minute undo window after cancellation

Canceling a booking is a stressful moment. The undo screen shows a live countdown right below the primary action. The Peak-End Rule from behavioral research says the last thing a user sees shapes how they remember the whole event. A generous exit ramp keeps the memory calm.

User Flow

07

The main flow covers first launch, role selection, sign in, service discovery, booking, and confirmation. Branches handle the caregiver path, reschedules, cancellations, and empty states where no pros are available nearby.

Visual Design

08

The visual language is built around two values. Warmth and legibility. A cream background (#FCF9F6) is paired with a deep burnt-brown primary (#702B00). The palette reads as domestic, not clinical. All interactive elements meet WCAG AA contrast. Body copy and category labels are set larger than typical mobile defaults, so the app is easy to read in daylight and with aging eyes.

High Fidelity Screens

09

Twenty-two screens cover the full senior journey, the caregiver dashboard, and the shared moments where both sides meet.

User Testing

10

Testing was done in person with three participants. Two seniors and one caregiver. Each session used ten decision-ready questions covering trust, pricing, status clarity, and what would cause them to stop using the app.

Testing Insights

11

Four findings. Four actions. Two that need to be fixed now. One that was validated and should stay as is. One new feature worth building next.

01

"Pending" status causes anxiety (fix now)

All three users misread "Pending" as a failed booking. Two tried to go back and start over. One showed clear stress. Action: change the label to "Awaiting David's confirmation" and add a note underneath, "David usually confirms within two hours."

02

"Call the pro" button needs one more line (fix now)

Both seniors asked the same question without being prompted. Will the pro actually pick up, or will it go to voicemail? Action: add a single supporting line under the button. "David usually responds within 15 minutes."

03

Specific trust credentials work (keep as is)

James (the skeptic) said he would google the license number to check it. Patricia (first-time app user) said the specific badge made her feel "like someone checked him." Two very different users, same trust outcome. Do not simplify the badge copy. The specificity is the mechanism.

04

Arrival confirmation is the biggest missing feature (build next)

Sandra brought it up without being asked. She wants a push notification that says "Elena arrived at 12:04 PM" with a one-tap call option if something feels wrong. That one note directly addresses the main caregiver fear. Not knowing if the pro actually showed up.

Outcomes & Reflection

12

Service Hub went from a blank page to a 26-screen iOS prototype across four rounds of revision. Every screen can be traced back to a research finding, a competitor gap, or a testing insight. Not one decision was made on taste alone.

The dual-customer approach held up. Designing two interfaces that share one trust layer is harder than designing one. It is also the thing that makes the product different from every competitor on the list. Trust specificity held up too. Generic "verified" badges did almost nothing in testing. License numbers, insurance carriers, and renewal dates did the real work.

The most useful lesson was about autonomy. The caregiver approval toggle could have been a default-on safety feature. That would have failed. Seniors would have felt supervised and walked away. Turning it into an opt-in gift from the senior to the caregiver turned the same feature into a reason to trust the product, not a reason to reject it.

Next steps are clear. Ship the four fixes from testing. Build arrival confirmation. Run a second round of testing with six participants. Design the provider side of the marketplace. Adapt for Canada, where 78% of seniors own their home and there is less platform competition.
