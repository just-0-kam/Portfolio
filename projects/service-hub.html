Service Hub — Senior-First Home Services Marketplace

Overview

00

A two-sided marketplace that connects senior citizens with vetted home service professionals — plumbers, electricians, HVAC technicians, painters, and more — through a mobile-first experience designed for low digital fluency.

Senior citizens represent a systematically underserved segment in the home services market. They need help frequently, yet the process of finding, vetting, and booking a contractor is fragmented, phone-dependent, and often mediated by family members. Service Hub was designed from the ground up to eliminate that dependency — giving seniors a dignified, self-sufficient path to getting the help they need.

The app acts as a transparent middleman: users browse services by category, view contractor profiles with ratings and hourly rates, book directly from the app, manage appointments in a unified booking view, and message contractors without ever needing to make a cold call.

Design Requirement: Every interaction must be navigable by a 74-year-old iPhone user with no prior experience in on-demand service apps — and it must feel trustworthy enough that they will do it again without help.

Problem Space

01

Three interlocking problems make hiring home service professionals unnecessarily difficult for seniors — and existing solutions are built for everyone except them.

01

Discovery Failure

Seniors rely exclusively on word-of-mouth referrals. If their social network doesn't know a good plumber, the search ends before it begins. There is no structured, trustworthy discovery channel accessible to this demographic.

02

Phone-Call Dependency

The current booking paradigm requires calling multiple companies, describing problems verbally, and negotiating times over the phone — a high-friction process that creates anxiety and discourages independent action.

03

Interface Exclusion

Existing on-demand service apps (TaskRabbit, Thumbtack) are designed for digitally native adults. Small text, low contrast, dense information architecture, and jargon-heavy flows exclude users who are unfamiliar with app conventions.

The JTBD framing: When a senior citizen needs a leaking tap fixed, they want to feel confident they've hired someone reliable without having to ask their children for help — and they want confirmation the job is scheduled so they can stop worrying about it.

Research & Users

02

Three real senior users were recruited for moderated usability testing. Their feedback exposed the gap between what the team assumed seniors needed and what they actually needed.

ZN

Zarina Nadir, 74 — iPhone user, app-familiar

Lived in Canada for 30 years, originally from Yemen. Constantly needs help around the house but relies purely on word of mouth. Independently motivated — wants to solve problems herself without asking family.

RB

Rozy Bharwani, 81 — iPhone owner, infrequent user

Has a part-time caretaker but still needs supplemental help. Typically delegates the search for contractors to her daughter. A strong candidate for the product — if the interface doesn't intimidate her into handing the phone away.

MG

Margaret, 72 — Proficient iPhone user

Comfortable with iOS apps. Relies on recommendations from friends and family. Brought the most granular UI critique — her feedback on contrast, icon clarity, and the booking flow was the most actionable data gathered.

Key Research Findings

Validated Hypotheses Invalidated Assumptions
  1. All three users confirmed immediate, genuine need — concept validation was unanimous.
  2. Bottom tab navigation was intuitive without instruction — the mental model mapped correctly.
  3. The Upcoming / Completed / Cancelled booking filter was praised as clear and useful.
  4. Skipping phone calls in favour of in-app contact resonated strongly with all users.
  1. The black-dominant colour scheme felt cold and hard to read — users wanted warmth.
  2. Category label text was too small; multiple users flagged it as a legibility barrier.
  3. Category icons were unclear — they failed at their core wayfinding job.
  4. Seniors preferred phone calls over messaging — the messaging-only model was insufficient.

Design Decisions

03

Every significant design decision maps to a specific constraint — senior accessibility, trust-building, or reduction of cognitive load.

01

Category-First Homepage Architecture

The homepage opens directly to a 3×4 category grid — no onboarding carousel, no promotional banners. Seniors arrive with a specific job in mind ("I need a plumber") — the UI immediately presents that vocabulary in their terms, not app jargon. A "Top Rated" section below mitigates discoverability loss for categories users don't know they need.

02

Contractor Profiles with Rating + Rate as Primary Data

Provider cards expose two trust signals above the fold: star rating and hourly price. For a senior who has historically relied on word-of-mouth, a star rating is the closest digital equivalent to a trusted recommendation. The hourly rate prevents the anxiety of "I don't know what this will cost."

03

Three-State Booking Management

The My Bookings screen uses a tab-based filter: Upcoming / Completed / Cancelled. Explicit "Reschedule" and "Cancel" actions on each card reduce error-recovery cost. A confirmation screen post-booking was identified as a missing moment — it would dramatically reduce post-booking anxiety for seniors prone to second-guessing.

04

Flat Settings Page (No Sub-Navigation)

The profile screen presents all options as a flat vertical list rather than nested menus. Depth is the enemy of senior navigation. Each item uses a single directional arrow — a universal iOS affordance that signals "tap to go deeper," reducing uncertainty.

05

OAuth-First Login (Google / Facebook)

The login screen leads with social authentication before presenting email/password fields. Password management is a significant cognitive burden for seniors — re-using weak passwords, forgetting credentials, and confusing "login" with "sign up" are common failure modes. OAuth delegates credential management to platforms seniors already trust.

06

Persistent Bottom Tab Bar (4 Core Functions)

Home, Bookings, Messages, and Profile are always visible at the bottom. The user never needs to remember where a feature lives. The 4-item tab count sits at the upper edge of Hick's Law comfort zone — adding a 5th item would measurably increase decision latency for this user group.

Usability Testing

04

Three moderated usability sessions were conducted with senior participants using the interactive Figma prototype on an iPhone. The test protocol included pre-test interviews and post-test structured debriefs to capture attitudinal data.

CRIT

Category text below icon grid is too small to read

Raised by Users 1 & 3. Response: increase label font size; explore bolding category names.

CRIT

Category icons are unrecognisable and lack colour

Raised by all 3 users. Response: replace with high-contrast, coloured, universally legible iconography.

CRIT

Booking form uses typed date/time input

Raised by User 3. Response: replace with native iOS date/time picker component.

CRIT

Yellow button-to-background contrast ratio below WCAG AA

Raised by User 3. Response: increase contrast ratio to meet minimum 4.5:1 for normal text.

HIGH

Black/white/gold colour scheme feels cold and uninviting

Raised by all 3 users. Response: introduce warmer palette; increase chromatic variety in UI.

HIGH

No app introduction on first launch

Raised by User 1. Response: add a brief onboarding screen explaining what the app does.

HIGH

Messaging-only contractor contact model is insufficient

Raised by all 3 users. Response: add direct "Call Contractor" button alongside messaging.

Highest-signal insight: Every single user validated the core concept without prompting — they said it would be beneficial to their daily lives. Product-market fit signal was strong. The problems were all execution-layer issues, not concept-level failures. That is an excellent place to be after a first prototype.

Outcomes & Iteration Plan

05

The implementation plan prioritises changes by their direct impact on the primary user's ability to complete their core job — booking a service — without assistance.

P1

Typography Scale

All body text, especially category labels, will be scaled up. Per WCAG 1.4.4, text must be resizable without loss of content. For this demographic, 14px is the minimum body size — the current design dips below it in category labels.

P1

Icon System Overhaul

The category icon grid will be rebuilt using high-contrast, colour-coded icons. Each icon will carry both a colour and a label — redundant coding, which is a proven accessibility technique for users with reduced visual acuity.

P1

Date / Time Picker

The free-text date and time input on the booking screen will be replaced with a native iOS wheel picker. This eliminates a major error-generation point — seniors should not have to format their own date strings.

P2

Direct Call Button

A "Call Contractor" CTA will sit alongside the message button on every provider card and chat screen. Voice is still the dominant trust modality for this demographic — the app needs to meet them where they are, not force a new behaviour.

P2

Colour & Contrast

The palette will shift toward a warmer, more chromatic system with verified WCAG AA contrast ratios across all interactive elements. The monochrome-heavy approach worked structurally but failed emotionally for this user group.

P3

Onboarding Screen

A single onboarding card on first launch will explain what Service Hub does in one sentence with a clear illustration. This addresses the "I forget what the app does" failure mode surfaced in testing.

Reflection

06

The most important lesson from Service Hub is the gap between assumed and actual accessibility. The team built a clean, minimal interface that — by conventional UX standards — was well-structured. Tab navigation was logical. Information hierarchy was clear. Contrast ratios felt sufficient. Then three 70-something users sat down with the prototype and revealed precisely where those assumptions collapsed.

Minimum viable contrast is not the same as appropriate contrast for aging eyes. Icon clarity for a 28-year-old designer is not the same as icon clarity for a 74-year-old user who has never used an on-demand service app. The category icon grid that felt self-evident in Figma was opaque in the hands of our actual users.

The other durable insight is about modality preference. The team defaulted to messaging as the primary contractor communication channel — it was simpler to prototype and aligned with how the team personally communicates. Every user in testing wanted a phone call option. Designing for your own mental model rather than your user's existing behaviour is one of the most common and most costly mistakes in UX. This project surfaced it early, which is exactly when you want to find it.

Structurally, the architecture held up. The booking management model was praised. The tab system was navigated correctly without instruction. The product concept generated genuine enthusiasm from every person who tested it. That is the right foundation. The next iteration will be better because the team knows precisely where to look.
